TRACK03

Sejong's Full Design

Modern Korean uses 24 letters. Sejong designed 28 — and more.

The 1446 Hunminjeongeum Haerye lists 28 letters: 17 consonants and 11 vowels. Four fell out of everyday Korean — three consonants (ㅿ ㆆ ㆁ) and one vowel (ㆍ). Korean didn't need them, so they faded. But the system was never limited to Korean: it also defines ways to combine letters into sounds no single letter covers. This section shows that toolbox, and how Hangulpia proposes to use it for Japanese, Mandarin and English.

雖風聲鶴唳 雞鳴狗吠 皆可得而書矣
"Even the sound of wind, the cry of a crane, a rooster’s crow, a dog’s bark — all can be written." — Jeong In-ji, preface to the Haerye
Source: Hunminjeongeum Haerye (1446)

TRACK04

Combination Rules

Letters are building blocks

The Haerye does not stop at 28 shapes. It gives rules for stacking, doubling and clustering them — which is how a small alphabet reaches a very large sound space.

Haerye letter/rule

1 · Light sounds (연서 連書)

ㅂ+ㅇ→ㅂㅍ+ㅇ→ㅍ
ㅁ+ㅇ→ㅁㄹ+ㅇ→ㄹ

Write ㅇ under a lip letter and the lips only barely touch: a fricative like [β] (ㅸ) instead of a full stop. Written under ㄹ it gives the "light tongue" sound, a quick tap.

Haerye: "ㅇ連書脣音之下，則為脣輕音". ㅸ is usually read as [β] (one scholar argues for /w/); the exact values of ㆄ ㅱ ㅹ and the tap value of ᄛ are scholarly interpretations.

Haerye letter/rule

2 · Doubling & clusters (병서 並書)

ㄱ+ㄱ→ㄲㅎ+ㅎ→ㆅ
ㅅ+ㄷ→ㅼup to 3 letters

Two of the same letter make a tense/voiced-type sound. Different letters can also be written together at the start of a syllable — two or three at once — so clusters like "str-" fit in one block with no filler vowel.

Haerye: "初聲二字三字合用並書".

Haerye letter/rule

3 · Any initial can be a final (종성부용초성)

가가가ㅂ

The Haerye says final consonants simply reuse the initial letters. So every new letter above can also close a syllable — which matters for Japanese small っ and for English endings like -v and -z.

Haerye letter/rule

4 · Pitch dots (방점 傍點)

가|·가|:가level · high · rising

Writing in the 1400s marked pitch with dots on the left of a syllable: none = level, one dot = high, two dots = rising. This is already a tone-marking system built into the script itself — useful for tonal languages like Mandarin.

Haerye letter/rule = uses a letter or rule found in the 1446 Haerye (which sound goes where is still our proposal)   Our extension = goes beyond the Haerye, extending its principles
TRACK05

Why it works

A phonetic alphabet, 442 years before the IPA

Most alphabets grew out of pictures: Latin letters descend from Egyptian hieroglyphs, and their shapes say nothing about how a sound is made. Hangul was designed the other way round. Each consonant is drawn from the place in the mouth where it is produced, and the Haerye explains the whole logic. The International Phonetic Alphabet, the linguists’ standard, was first published in 1888 (its association was founded in Paris in 1886). Hunminjeongeum was promulgated in 1446. Linguist Geoffrey Sampson called Hangul a “featural” alphabet for this reason: its letter shapes encode sound features.

The Haerye also pairs each place of articulation with one of the five elements, tying the sounds of speech into the cosmology of its day.

  1. 1443

    Sejong creates 28 letters

    The Annals record that the king created 28 letters that could write both Chinese-character words and native speech.

  2. 1446

    Hunminjeongeum promulgated

    The Haerye explains how the letters were designed, including the combination rules above.

  3. 1447–48

    Dongguk Jeongun

    A dictionary that fixes the standard reading of Chinese characters in Hangul. Only two of its six volumes survive. Later critics found it artificial, and its system was dropped by the end of the 1400s.

  4. 1455

    Hongmu Jeongun Yeokhun

    Standard Chinese pronunciation written in Hangul — direct evidence that Hangul was used to transcribe a foreign language.

  5. 1888

    The IPA is first published

    Europe’s linguists agree on a shared phonetic alphabet, built from Latin letters.

  6. 1933

    24 letters fixed

    The Hangul Spelling Unification Plan settles on the modern 24 letters. The lost letters were not needed for Korean, so they were dropped.

This section was inspired in part by a public lecture, “Why is Hangul considered a world-class script? — Design House Hunminjeongeum lecture”. Claims were re-checked against other sources where possible; statements we could not verify are left out.

TRACK06

Beyond Korean

What the 24-letter version cannot write

Today, Japanese, Mandarin and English all get squeezed through the 24 modern letters: ざ and さ collapse, r and l merge, f and p merge. Sejong’s lost letters and combination rules fit many of these gaps surprisingly well. Below is our proposed notation for each language, with every row marked as historical or as our own extension.

Honest status: this is a research proposal. The letters and combination rules are Sejong’s; the sound-to-letter assignments for Japanese, Mandarin and English are our design, to be tested with linguists and native speakers. The downloadable app and the converter below do not use these letters yet — they still output ordinary hiragana from the 24 modern letters.

TRACK07

Can you type it today?

The letters exist. The keyboard does not.

Good news: Unicode already encodes almost every letter above, so nothing new has to be standardized for most of them. Bad news: a standard Korean keyboard has no key for any of them, and the Hangulpia app cannot produce them yet.

Current limits

  • No default keys. Standard Dubeolsik has no key for ㅿ ㆆ ㆁ ㆍ or the light-sound letters. We could not confirm any built-in way to type them on macOS.
  • No precomposed syllables. Unicode precomposes only the 11,172 modern syllables. A syllable like "ㅿ+ㅏ" must be built from conjoining jamo, which needs a font and renderer that support it. Community reports mention broken display in some browsers.
  • Two kinds of jamo. Compatibility jamo (ㅿ U+317F, …) are standalone and do not combine into syllables; conjoining jamo combine but look different when alone.
  • Our engine is modern-Hangul only. The decoder and its training table are built on the 24 modern letters, so extended letters are simply not part of what it handles today.
  • New clusters have no key or code. ð (ㅿ+ㄷ) is our own cluster. ㅅㄷ exists in Unicode, but as the old s+t cluster, not as θ.
  • Typing cost. The Windows layout leans on Shift for rare letters, and its own guide warns of frequent typos.

Where we want to take it

Try it in the browser first

Add an on-screen extended-jamo keypad to the homepage practice box (ㅿ ㆆ ㆁ ㆍ ㅸ ㆄ ᄛ). It needs no OS support, works on any device, and our SVG glyphs sidestep missing fonts.

Make the engine read them

Add deterministic rules ahead of the statistical map: ㅿ→ざ-row, final ㆆ→っ, ㆁ→nasal が, ᄛ→ら-row, ㆄ→ふ. This directly attacks today's weakest spots (voicing, small っ) and could replace the ~ and ^ markers.

A typing layer in the macOS app

Reuse the familiar Windows Shift layout where it does not clash, and put the clashing ones (like Shift+ㅅ=ㅆ) on an Option layer. Output stays kana, so no font is needed for the result.

Teach the model extended notation

Extend the kana→Hangul table that generates training pairs so it can also emit the extended letters, then retrain the PhoneticMap. Ambiguity should fall because ざ/さ and ら/ラ stop sharing one Hangul spelling.

Validate & standardize

Test the assignments with linguists and native speakers. For ð and other new clusters, decide how they are typed and encoded.

Live Now

First up: Japanese

Type on your regular Dubeolsik keyboard, get real-time Hiragana. A statistical engine trained on a 4,153-word vocabulary, plus explicit grammar rules for particles and verb endings, fills in what a plain rulebook alone would miss.

  • Real-time conversion — Dubeolsik in, Hiragana out, no retraining needed
  • Statistically trained — a 4,153-word vocabulary plus explicit particle/conjugation grammar rules catch what character-level statistics alone miss
  • Manual override markers — ~ for long vowels, ^ for dakuten, for the cases statistics alone gets wrong
  • Built for macOS — native InputMethodKit app, ready today. Windows is next.

TRACK09

Setup

Up and running in 3 minutes

No App Store, so macOS needs a few extra steps to trust it. Do these once, in order.

1

Unzip

Double-click the downloaded file in your Downloads folder.

2

Run the installer

Open Terminal, drag the unzipped folder onto the window to jump there, then run:

terminal
bash install.sh
3

Log out, log back in

This is how macOS notices the new keyboard exists. A restart also works.

4

Add the input source

System Settings → Keyboard → Input Sources → Edit → +, find Japanese, add HangulJapaneseIME.

5

Switch and type

Click the input source icon in the menu bar, pick it, and type Dubeolsik like always.

Want it gone? Same folder, run bash uninstall.sh.

TRACK10

Fine control

When the engine guesses wrong, correct it

The engine is statistical, not a fixed rulebook — so it occasionally misses a long vowel or picks the unvoiced version of a consonant. Two markers let you override it, syllable by syllable, without retraining anything.

~

Long vowel (장음)

Attach right after a syllable to stretch its vowel sound.

도→ど
도~→どう
^

Dakuten (탁음)

Attach right after a syllable to force its voiced consonant (か→が, さ→ざ, た→だ, は→ば).

카→か
카^→が

Stack both on the same syllable: 카^~ → があ. Everything else in the sentence still goes through the trained engine as usual — markers only touch the syllable they're attached to.

What's next?
